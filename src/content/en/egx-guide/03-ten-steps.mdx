## Step 1: Get your foundation in place

Before buying a single share, three things should be true:

- **You have an emergency fund****—three** to six months of essential expenses, held in cash or a money market instrument. Money in shares is not money you can rely on next week.
- **You have no high-interest debt.** Paying down on expensive borrowing is a guaranteed return; the stock market offers no guarantees.
- **You are investing money you will not need for at least three to five years.** Short horizons expose you to the risk of selling at a loss simply because you needed the cash.

Then define what you are investing for. Retirement in 25 years, a home deposit in 7, a child's education in 12; the goal sets the horizon, and the horizon sets how much risk is appropriate.

## Step 3: Fund the account

Cash moves into your trading account through the app's wallet, typically by bank transfer. Transfer only what you intend to invest. Funds sit in your account as cash until you use them to buy shares, and remain yours to withdraw at any time, subject to settlement of any open trades.

There is no meaningful minimum. A single share is the smallest unit you can buy, and many EGX-listed shares trade at prices that make a first purchase accessible.

## Step 4: Learn to read a stock

Every share on the exchange has a set of numbers attached to it. The ones that matter first:

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Ticker | The short code that identifies the share (e.g., COMI for Commercial International Bank). |
| Last price | The price at which the share most recently traded. |
| Bid / Ask | The highest price a buyer is currently offering / the lowest price a seller is currently accepting. The gap is the spread. |
| Volume | The number of shares traded in the session. Higher volume means easier buying and selling. |
| Market capitalization | Share price × total shares outstanding of the stock. The total market value of the company. |
| Earnings per share (EPS) | Net profits divided by total shares outstanding. |
| Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) | Share price ÷ EPS. A rough measure of how much the market pays for each pound of profit. |
| Dividend yield | Annual dividend per share ÷ share price, expressed as a percentage. |
| 52-week high/low | The highest and lowest prices over the past year. Context for where the price sits today. |

None of these numbers answers the question "should I buy?" on its own. They are inputs to a judgement, not substitutes for one.

## Step 5: Build a watchlist and do your research

Start with companies you understand, businesses whose products you use, whose industry you follow, or whose model you can explain in a sentence. Add them to a watchlist and observe them for a few weeks before committing money.

There are two broad approaches to analyzing a share:

**Fundamental analysis** asks whether the business is worth owning. It looks at revenue and profit growth, debt levels, margins, competitive position, and management quality, then compares the share price to that underlying value. Company financial statements, published quarterly and annually on the EGX website and in the company's investor relations section, are the primary source.

**Technical analysis** asks what the price is likely to do next based on how it has behaved in the past. It uses charts, trends, and trading volume rather than financial statements. It is more commonly used for shorter-term decisions.

First-time investors are usually better served by fundamentals. A business that grows its earnings over a decade tends to reward patient owners regardless of what the chart looked like in any given month.

## Step 6: Place your first order

An order is an instruction to your broker to buy or sell a specific number of shares under specific conditions. The two main types:

- **Market order**: buy or sell immediately at the best available price. Fast, but you accept whatever price the market offers at that moment. In thinly traded shares, that price can differ from what you saw on screen.
- **Limit order**: buy or sell only at a price you specify, or better. You control the price; you don't control whether the order executes. If the market never reaches your limit, the order goes unfilled.

For a first trade, a limit order is the safer choice. It removes the risk of an unexpected fill.

Each order also carries validity: a day order expires at the close of the session if not filled; a good-till-cancelled order stays open until it executes, or you withdraw it.

The EGX applies **daily price limits** to listed shares. If a share moves beyond a set percentage from its previous close, typically 10% on the Main Market, trading is paused and, in some cases, the limit is widened. The limit prevents disorderly price swings; you will encounter it rarely, but it explains why a share sometimes stops moving mid-session.

To place the order on EFG Hermes One: select the share, choose to buy or sell, enter the quantity and price (for a limit order), set the validity, and confirm. The app shows the order's status- pending, partially filled, filled, or cancelled, in real time. Orders placed outside trading hours queue and are submitted at the next pre-opening session.

## Step 7: Understand settlement

Buying a share and owning it are separated by two business days. The EGX settles on a **T+2** basis: a trade executed on Sunday settles on Tuesday; a trade executed on Thursday settles the following Monday.

In practice:

- When you **buy**, the shares are formally recorded in your name at MCDR on the settlement date. Your broker typically lets you sell them before then, since both trades settle in the same cycle.
- When you **sell**, the cash arrives in your account on the settlement date. Until then, it appears as unsettled proceeds, usually available to reinvest, but not yet available to withdraw.

Settlement also determines dividend eligibility. To receive a dividend, you must be the registered owner on the **record date**, which means buying at least two business days before it.

## Step 8: Know the costs

Every trade carries costs. They are small individually and add up with frequent trading.

- **Brokerage commission**, a percentage of the trade value charged by your broker. Rates are published in the app and in the broker's fee schedule.
- **Regulatory and exchange fees**, small levies collected on behalf of the EGX, MCDR, the FRA, and the investor protection fund, expressed as fractions of a percent of trade value.
- **Stamp duty**: Following the 2026 amendments to Egypt's tax framework, capital gains on EGX-listed securities are exempt from income tax and subject instead to a proportional stamp duty on transaction value, split between buyer and seller. The rate is set by the Stamp Duty Law and applied automatically at execution.
- **Dividend withholding tax**: dividends from EGX-listed companies are subject to a flat withholding tax, deducted before the cash reaches your account.

Tax treatment changes. The figures above reflect the framework in force at the time of writing; confirm current rates in the app or with a tax adviser before relying on them.

## Step 9: Manage your portfolio

Your first share is a starting point, not a strategy. The principles that turn a collection of holdings into a portfolio:

**Diversify.** Do not concentrate your capital on one company or one sector. A bank, a telecom operator, a consumer goods company, and a real estate developer respond to different economic forces, holding all four smooths your returns. Ten to fifteen well-chosen positions across several sectors are a reasonable target for a retail portfolio.

**Size positions deliberately.** Decide in advance what share of your portfolio any single company can represent; 10% is a common ceiling and stick to it.

**Reinvest dividends.** Cash dividends left idle earn nothing. Reinvesting them, whether in the same share or elsewhere, is where compounding does its work.

**Track corporate actions.** Companies issue new shares (rights issues), split existing ones, distribute bonus shares, and occasionally get acquired. Each affect what you own. The EGX publishes announcements; your broker relays the ones that affect your holdings.

**Rebalance periodically.** A share that doubles becomes a larger part of your portfolio than you intended. Once or twice a year, review your allocation and trim what has grown out of proportion.

**Keep records.** Note what you bought, when, at what price, and why. The "why" is the part most investors forget, and the part that tells you, months later, whether your reasoning held.

## Step 10: Manage risk and yourself

Markets punish predictable errors. The ones first-timers do most often:

- **Buying on a tip.** A friend, a forum, a social media account- none of them bears the consequences of your trade. If you cannot explain why a share is worth owning, do not own it.
- **Chasing what just went up.** A share that rose 30% last month is not a reason to buy; it is a reason to ask whether the rise was justified.
- **Selling on the first decline.** Volatility is the price of admission. A 10% drop in the share you researched and still believe in is not a signal; it is noise.
- **Checking prices constantly.** Daily movements carry almost no information about long-term value. Look at your portfolio weekly or monthly, not hourly.
- **Trading too much.** Every trade costs money and invites a decision you might get wrong. The best portfolios are often the least touched.
- **Using leverage before you are ready.** Margin trading amplifies losses as well as gains. Leave it until you have a track record of disciplined decisions with your own money.

Set rules before you need them: how much you will invest, how you will decide what to buy, and what would make you sell. Write them down. When the market tests your resolve, and it will, the written rule outlasts the emotion of the moment.
