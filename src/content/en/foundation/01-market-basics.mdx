## The market is a machine that runs on fear and greed

Every price move tells a story about human psychology. When greed dominates, people chase rising prices and buy recklessly. When fear dominates, they panic sell at the worst moment. The market doesn't respond to facts — it responds to how people *feel* about facts.

> Be greedy when others are fearful. Be fearful when others are greedy.
> — Warren Buffett

Sentiment indices measure this in real time. A reading above 50 means investors are leaning greedy. Below 50, fearful. During COVID-2020, the index crashed to extreme fear. During the Bitcoin peak of 2021, it hit extreme greed. Both were turning points.

<Callout type="note">
The five perspectives below represent different schools of thought about what the market actually is. None of them is completely right. All of them are useful.
</Callout>

---

### Perspective 1 — Some see only equations

Quantitative traders strip out all the narrative. To them, a stock is just a time series — patterns, autocorrelations, mean reversion, momentum signals. The market becomes a mathematical system with exploitable inefficiencies. Find the signal in the noise before others do, and you win.

```
Price(t)     = f(momentum, volatility)
Signal       = MovingAvg(t) − MovingAvg(t−n)
Mean Rev.    = P(return to μ) → high when far from mean
Risk         = σ × √(time) × position_size
─────────────────────────────────────────────
EDGE         = Expected Value > 0
```

### Perspective 2 — Or maybe it's just beautiful chaos

The Efficient Market Hypothesis argues that all known information is already priced in. No analysis can consistently beat the market, because prices move randomly in response to new, unpredictable information. If this is true, the best strategy is: own everything, pay nothing in fees, and wait.

Most active funds lose to the index over 10 years. This is not a fringe view — it's the dominant conclusion of decades of academic research.

### Perspective 3 — It's an information game

Markets are a competition between participants with asymmetric information. Insiders know earnings before you do. Big institutions pay for satellite imagery, credit card transaction flows, and sentiment data feeds. Fast traders see your order before it fully executes. The edge is always about information — its quality, speed, and interpretation.

### Perspective 4 — Your job: find the signal inside the noise

Whatever your philosophy — behavioral, quantitative, or somewhere in between — trading is ultimately about separating real, exploitable patterns from random fluctuations. The sessions ahead give you the foundation to start.

## 01 — What Is the Stock Market?

At its core, a stock market is a marketplace where buyers and sellers trade ownership pieces of companies.

**A company needs capital.** Instead of borrowing from a bank, it can sell small pieces of itself — called shares — to the public.

**You buy a share.** When you buy a share, you own a tiny fraction of that company. If the company does well, your share is worth more. If it does badly, it's worth less.

**The Exchange (EGX).** The Egyptian Exchange is the regulated marketplace where all this buying and selling happens in real time, under FRA supervision.

**The Broker (EFG).** You can't walk into EGX and trade directly. You need a licensed intermediary — EFG Hermes — to place orders on your behalf.

### The Trading Flow

From investor intention to executed trade:

**Investor** → wants to buy shares  
**Broker (EFG)** → places the order  
**Exchange (EGX)** → matches buyer and seller  
**Settlement** → shares and cash transfer (T+2 on EGX)

<Callout type="note">
T+2 means the actual transfer of shares and cash happens two business days after the trade executes. You can see the trade immediately — but ownership officially changes hands 48 hours later.
</Callout>

## 02 — How Is a Stock Price Formed?

Price is simply what the last buyer agreed to pay the last seller. Supply versus demand in real time.

When more people want to buy than sell, price rises. When more want to sell than buy, price falls.

### What Moves a Stock Price?

| Factor | Example | Typical Effect |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| Earnings Report | Company profits beat expectations | ↑ Price rises |
| Interest Rates | Central bank raises rates | ↓ Stocks fall |
| Macroeconomic News | GDP growth stronger than expected | ↑ Market rallies |
| Currency Moves | EGP depreciates sharply | ↓ Mixed impact |
| Sector News | New real estate law passed | ~ Sector rotation |
| Company Scandal | CEO resigns unexpectedly | ↓ Price drops |

## 03 — How Do You Buy or Sell?

When you trade, you place an order — an instruction to buy or sell. Different order types give you different control over price versus speed.

**Market Order** — Buy now at whatever the market offers. Guarantees execution, not price. Used when speed matters more than precision.

**Limit Order** — Only buy at EGP 65 or lower. Guarantees price, not execution. The trade only happens if the market reaches your target.

**Stop-Loss Order** — If price falls to EGP 55, automatically sell to cut losses. Critical for risk management — lets you define your worst case before you enter a trade.

**Stop-Limit Order** — Triggered at the stop price, but only executes within your limit range. More control than a stop-loss, but can miss in fast-moving markets.

<Callout type="note">
The order book is the real-world version of the supply vs. demand concept above — captured at a single moment in time, at actual price levels, with real quantities waiting to trade. Bid side = buyers. Ask side = sellers. The gap between them is the spread.
</Callout>

## 04 — What Is a Price Chart?

A price chart is a visual record of a stock's price over time. It's the primary tool traders use to understand market behaviour. Two chart types matter most.

### The Line Chart

The simplest price chart. Connects only the closing prices of each period — one dot per day (or hour, or minute), joined by a line.

Good for: seeing the big-picture trend, long-term analysis, cutting through noise.  
Limitation: throws away everything that happened during the period — you don't see open, high, or low.

### The Japanese Candlestick

Invented in 18th-century Japan by rice trader Munehisa Homma. Each candle packs four data points into one visual: Open, High, Low, Close.

The body shows open-to-close. The wicks (thin lines above and below) show the highest and lowest price reached during that period. **The wicks are where the story is.**

- **Green candle** — close was higher than open. Buyers were in control.
- **Red candle** — close was lower than open. Sellers were in control.
- **Long wick above** — price reached up but was rejected. Selling pressure.
- **Long wick below** — price reached down but bounced. Buying support.

### Volume

Volume measures how many shares changed hands in a period. It answers: how much conviction was behind this price move?

A large price move on high volume is a strong signal. The same move on low volume is weak and likely to reverse. Price and volume are two separate dimensions of information — neither tells the full story alone.

## 05 — Who Trades in the Market?

The market has many different types of participants — each with different goals, time horizons, and strategies.

| Participant | Who They Are | Time Horizon | Strategy |
|------------|--------------|--------------|----------|
| Retail Investor | Individual trading their own money | Days → Years | Varied — value, growth, momentum |
| Institutional Investor | Mutual funds, pension funds | Months → Years | Fundamental analysis, portfolio construction |
| Market Maker | Firms that always stand ready to buy AND sell | Seconds → Minutes | Earn the bid-ask spread; manage inventory risk |
| Algo / Quant Trader | Uses code to execute strategies automatically | Milliseconds → Days | Math-based signals, fast execution, statistical edges |
| Regulator (FRA / EGX) | Ensures fairness and transparency | Ongoing | Rules, circuit breakers, disclosure requirements |

### Market Makers on EGX

EGX launched its formal Market Maker program in January 2019. Today, only one firm is registered — Beltone Market Maker (code 5095). In mature markets like the US, dozens of market makers compete on every liquid stock, which is part of why their spreads stay tight and liquidity is deep. On EGX, most liquidity comes from natural buyers and sellers meeting in the order book — one reason spreads can widen and liquidity can dry up faster on news.

Egyptian retail investors dominate at approximately 82% of all transactions. Institutional and foreign participation remains low. *(Source: EGX / Zawya, 2025)*

## 06 — Key Terms

Essential vocabulary you'll encounter constantly. Know these before Session 2.

<Term>Bull Market</Term> — A market trending upward — prices rising 20%+ from recent lows. Optimism dominates.

<Term>Bear Market</Term> — A market trending downward — prices falling 20%+ from recent highs. Pessimism dominates.

<Term>Liquidity</Term> — How easily you can buy or sell without moving the price. High liquidity = easy to trade in size.

<Term>Volatility</Term> — How much price fluctuates. High volatility = bigger swings = more risk and more opportunity.

<Term>Bid / Ask Spread</Term> — Bid = highest price buyers offer. Ask = lowest price sellers accept. The spread is the market maker's margin.

<Term>Market Cap</Term> — Total value of a company = share price × number of shares outstanding.

<Term>Dividend</Term> — A portion of company profits paid directly to shareholders. Not all companies pay dividends.

<Term>Short Selling</Term> — Borrowing shares to sell now, hoping to buy them back cheaper later. A bet on price decline.

<Term>IPO</Term> — Initial Public Offering — when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time.
