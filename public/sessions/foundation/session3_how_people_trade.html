01 — WHY PEOPLE TRADE

Three Reasons People Are in the Market

On any given day, the same stock is being bought by people with completely different goals. Knowing which goal you have shapes everything — your time horizon, how much risk you can take, what success even looks like. Most people lose money because they confuse one for another.

Goal 01
Build Wealth
You buy good companies and hold them for years — sometimes decades. The goal is to let compounding do the work. You don't care about today's price; you care about where the company is in 10 years.
Time horizon: Years to decades
Effort: Low (after research)
Win rate: High over long periods
Goal 02
Generate Income
You buy companies that pay regular dividends — banks, utilities, mature businesses. The goal is steady cash flow, not capital gains. Price moves matter less than the dividend cheque.
Time horizon: Years
Effort: Low to medium
Win rate: Stable, modest
Goal 03
Speculate
You're trying to profit from short-term price moves — minutes, days, weeks. You don't care about the company; you care about whether the chart is going up or down right now. This is what most people picture when they hear "trading."
Time horizon: Seconds to weeks
Effort: Very high
Win rate: Most people lose money
↳ The most common mistake
People open accounts to build wealth and end up speculating by accident. They buy a stock for the long term, see it drop 5%, panic-sell, then buy something else hoping for a quick rebound. That's not investing — that's expensive emotional trading. Pick your goal before you pick the trade.
02 — TIME HORIZONS

The Spectrum: From Decades to Seconds

"Trading" is a single word that covers wildly different activities. Holding Coca-Cola for 30 years is trading. Buying and selling a stock in 4 seconds is also trading. They share almost nothing else in common — different costs, different skills, different evidence of what works.

YEARS — DECADES
Investing
Buy and hold. Lowest cost per decision. Compounding does the heavy lifting. Best long-term win rate of any approach.
WEEKS — MONTHS
Position Trading
Riding longer market trends. Mix of fundamentals and technicals. Few trades per year but each is meaningful.
DAYS — WEEKS
Swing Trading
Catching short-term swings. Pure technicals usually. You sleep with positions, ride them for a few days, exit on a chart signal.
MINUTES — HOURS
Day Trading
Open and close positions same day. Never holds overnight. Full-time job. High emotional load, high transaction costs.
SECONDS — MINUTES
Scalping
Hundreds of trades a day, each holding for seconds. Razor-thin per-trade profit. In retail, this is almost always a losing game once costs are included.

Active vs Passive — the bigger fork

Across the entire spectrum above, there's an even more fundamental choice: try to beat the market (active) or try to match the market (passive). Session 2 already showed you the punchline — over 20 years, ~95% of professional active managers lose to the index. Yet most retail investors still try to beat it. Here's why both choices are defensible:

Passive
Buy the whole market. Forget about it.
You buy an index ETF (e.g. EGX30 ETF, S&P500 ETF) and let it run. You accept the average market return — but you also pay almost nothing in fees, make almost no decisions, and don't have to predict anything. Boring on purpose. Wins by not losing.
Active
Pick what to buy. Try to beat average.
You research, decide, time entries and exits. The reward when you're right is bigger than the index — but the cost of being wrong is brutal. Most people who try this lose to the index over time, but a small number genuinely beat it. Hard to know in advance which group you're in.
03 — RISK MANAGEMENT

The Most Important Section in This Whole Series

If you remember nothing else from these 5 sessions, remember this: surviving long enough to learn is the entire game. Most people who lose at trading don't lose because they picked bad stocks — they lose because one bad trade was big enough to wipe out twenty good ones. Risk management is what stops that from happening.

Rule 01
Position Sizing
Never put more than a small fraction of your account on any single trade. Most professionals follow some version of the 1% rule — risk no more than 1% of your total account on any one position. If you have 100,000 EGP, the most you should be willing to lose on a single trade is 1,000.
Rule 02
Stop-Losses
Decide your exit price before you enter the trade. The moment price hits your stop, you exit — no negotiation with yourself. The single biggest mistake retail investors make is moving the stop further away when it's about to trigger. That's how a small loss becomes a catastrophic one.
Rule 03
Diversification
Don't put it all in one stock. Don't put it all in one sector. Don't put it all in one country. The whole point is to reduce the chance that one bad event takes you out of the game. Diversification doesn't maximise returns — it minimises catastrophe.
Rule 04
Risk-Reward Ratio
Before every trade, ask: how much can I lose if I'm wrong, vs. how much can I make if I'm right? Most professionals won't take a trade unless the upside is at least 2x the downside. You can be right less than half the time and still make money — if your winners are bigger than your losers.
↳ The math that surprises everyone
Losses hurt more than gains help. If you lose 50% on a trade, you don't need 50% to get back to even — you need 100%. The deeper the hole, the harder it is to climb out:

This is why protecting your capital is so much more important than chasing big wins. A trader who never lets a single loss exceed 5–10% of their position can survive almost any market. A trader who lets one trade go to -50% may never recover.
04 — BEHAVIOURAL BIASES

Your Brain Is the Enemy

Markets don't beat you — your own decisions beat you. Decades of behavioural finance research have shown that humans have predictable, repeatable mental shortcuts that hurt their trading. Knowing these biases doesn't make you immune. But it gives you a fighting chance to catch yourself.

Bias 01
Loss Aversion
Losing 1,000 EGP feels about 2x as painful as winning 1,000 EGP feels good. So people hold losers hoping they'll recover (avoiding the pain of realising the loss) and sell winners too early (locking in the pleasure). Both behaviours are exactly backwards.
Bias 02
Confirmation Bias
Once you own a stock, you start seeking out news that supports your view and ignoring news that contradicts it. Bullish on CIB? You'll read every positive analyst report and dismiss the negative ones as biased. Your brain is now your stock's PR agent, not its analyst.
Bias 03
Recency Bias
Whatever just happened feels like the new normal. After three good days, you assume the trend is up forever. After three bad days, you assume the world is ending. Markets don't care about your last three days — but your brain weights them like they're everything.
Bias 04
Disposition Effect
A specific consequence of loss aversion: investors sell winners too quickly and hold losers too long. The result is a portfolio of underperforming stocks, while the strong ones — which should compound — were sold months ago. The market rewards the opposite behaviour.
Bias 05
Overconfidence
Most traders rate themselves "above average" — which is statistically impossible. After a few good trades, people assume they have skill. They trade more, take bigger positions, and skip risk management. The market eventually delivers the lesson, often expensively.
Bias 06
Herd Mentality
When everyone is buying, you feel stupid not buying. When everyone is selling, you feel stupid not selling. The crowd is right most of the time — but at the extremes (peaks and crashes), the crowd is catastrophically wrong. Following them at those moments is how bubbles trap people.
05 — THE LOSERS' GAME

An Honest Picture of Retail Trading

Time for the part nobody wants to say out loud: most retail traders lose money. Not some. Most. The numbers are remarkably consistent across markets, decades, and methodologies. This isn't a deterrent — it's the most useful thing you can know before you start.

↳ What the research consistently shows
~70-80%
of active retail day traders lose money over any given year. Most studies on Brazil, Taiwan, US retail brokerages converge on this range.
~95%
of professional active fund managers underperform their benchmark over 20 years (S&P SPIVA Scorecard). If pros struggle, what does that say about the rest of us?
~1%
of day traders are consistently profitable over multi-year periods (Brazilian study, 2020). Possible — but rare.
Sources: S&P SPIVA Scorecard · Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti (2020) on Brazilian day traders

Why most lose

Costs add up
Commissions, spreads, taxes, opportunity cost. Active trading bleeds 1–3% per year before you've even made a decision. Index funds bleed almost nothing.
No edge
If you don't know why you'd consistently win against people who do this full-time with better tools, you probably won't.
Bias accumulation
Every bias from the previous section compounds. Loss aversion + overconfidence + recency bias = a perfect storm of bad decisions, repeated.
Survivorship bias
You hear about people who got rich trading. You don't hear about the much larger number who quietly went broke. The visible winners aren't a representative sample.

What the survivors do differently

Obsess over risk
Every successful trader you'll meet talks about losses more than wins. Not because they enjoy it — because controlling them is what kept them in the game long enough to win.
Have a process, not opinions
Every entry, exit, and position size has a documented reason that exists before they click. Discretion is the enemy of consistency.
Trade less
More trades = more transaction costs and more chances to make a mistake. The worst-performing retail accounts are almost always the most active ones.
Stay humble
They assume they're going to be wrong a lot. They size positions and set stops as if every trade will lose. The wins take care of themselves.
↳ The honest takeaway
The point of this session isn't to scare you off the market — it's to make sure you're playing the game you actually want to play. If you want to build wealth, the evidence overwhelmingly favours buying broad index ETFs and holding for decades. If you want to trade actively, do it with eyes open: small position sizes, strict risk management, and a process you trust more than your gut. The next two sessions will give you the tools — technical and fundamental — that active traders use to make those decisions.
